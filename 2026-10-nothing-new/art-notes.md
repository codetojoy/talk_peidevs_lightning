Willem Claesz. Heda, Still Life with a Gilt Cup (1635)

* Dutch Baroque

A near-monochrome "tonal banquet piece": mostly grays, with a few yellow and ochre accents.
The skill is in surfaces: pewter, silver, damask, glass and mother-of-pearl, each rendered with its own sheen.
One fixed viewpoint, no visible brushstrokes, perfect illusion.
Talking point: "This is the form, the version everyone learned."

Paul Cézanne, The Basket of Apples (c. 1893)

* early Renaissance
* multiple-points at once
* not trying to represent realism
* not showing technical mastery: innovation

Same ingredients as Heda: table, fruit, bottle, cloth.
The right side of the tabletop doesn't line up with the left, as if seen from two viewpoints at once.
The bottle tilts and the basket leans forward, yet the whole still balances.
Talking point: "Still a still life, with one deliberate 'wrong' choice. This kind of painting became a bridge to Cubism."

Henri Matisse, The Dessert: Harmony in Red (1908)

* flatness
* divorcing colour from reality: lighting

One pattern covers both the wall and the table, so the table's edge nearly disappears.
No conventional focal point and a flattened perspective.
The background started green, became blue for its 1908 exhibition, and was repainted red in 1909.
Talking point: "Same composition, three different color schemes. He kept reworking his own idea."

Roy Lichtenstein, Still Life with Crystal Bowl (1972)

* pop artist
* three primary colours
* comic book

The most traditional subject of the four: a bowl of fruit on a table.
Bold outlines, flat colors, and printing dots make it look machine-printed, but it's oil and acrylic on canvas.
It bookends Heda: both paint reflections on glass, Heda through illusion and Lichtenstein through graphic shorthand.
Talking point: "Same subject, completely new technique."

Wrap-up: Heda perfected the form, Cézanne bent the viewpoint, Matisse flattened it into color and pattern, and Lichtenstein changed how it's made. The table and the fruit never left.
